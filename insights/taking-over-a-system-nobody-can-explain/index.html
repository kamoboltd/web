Taking over a system nobody can explain

James Dettmer, Principal 4 November 2026 2 min read

Inheriting a platform is a research job first and an engineering job second. Budget for the reading.

A particular kind of call comes in every few months. The system works, mostly. The people who built it have gone. There is some documentation and it is out of date. Something needs to change and nobody is confident enough to change it.

This is its own discipline, and the mistake is to treat it as a normal engineering engagement that happens to have a slow start.

Read before you touch

On the streaming platform we took over, the first phase produced no commits at all. We reverse-engineered the microservice architecture and, separately, the build and deploy process, because those two things had drifted apart and neither matched the documentation.

The deploy process is usually the more revealing of the two. Architecture diagrams describe intent. Pipelines describe what actually happens, including the manual step somebody added at two in the morning three years ago that is now load-bearing.

Audit the infrastructure for what it is, not what it says

Next is capacity: what is actually provisioned, against what is actually used. On that platform this turned up components over-provisioned and under-provisioned at the same time, which is the normal state of an estate that has been tuned reactively over years. Each individual decision made sense when it was made. Collectively they make no sense at all.

We did the same on a 250-staff law firm's VMware estate, running an RVtools audit out of hours before proposing anything, then sizing a target cluster from the measurements rather than from the existing specification. Consolidating three VMware environments into one Nutanix AHV cluster plus Azure services was the outcome, but the audit was the thing that made the proposal defensible.

Standardise before you improve

Inherited estates are rarely uniform. On the streaming platform we reverse-engineered an existing Puppet codebase, upgraded Puppet Server, wrote new modules, and then migrated from Puppet modules to Ansible playbooks, standardising the Linux virtual machines as we went.

That sequence matters. Migrating configuration management while the configuration itself is inconsistent means you encode the inconsistency into the new tool and now have two problems. Get the estate uniform under the tool you have, then change tools.

What to budget

The research phase is a real line item and it is the one clients most often want to compress. It is also the phase that determines whether everything after it is correct. A few days of reading, measuring and interviewing is cheap against the cost of re-architecting the wrong component.

Our Azure Review exists for this shape of problem: three days, read-only, and a written answer to what is actually wrong with the estate rather than what somebody assumes is wrong.

Leave it explicable

The last obligation is not to recreate the problem. Infrastructure as code in the client's own repositories, documented, with the reasoning written down and not just the result. If you hand back a system that only you understand, you have moved the dependency rather than removed it.

Questions this usually raises

How long does the research phase take?

Days rather than weeks for a single platform. The variable is not the size of the estate so much as how many people are still available to ask, and how much of the deployment process is automated versus remembered.

Can you do this without touching production?

The research phase, yes, and it should be read-only. The point of separating it from the engineering is that you can commit to the first part before anyone has to agree what the second part is.

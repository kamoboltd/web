Cost tools cannot tell you what nobody needs

James Dettmer, Principal 4 November 2026 3 min read

An audit tells you what is running and what it costs. It cannot tell you whether anyone still needs it, and that is where the rest of the money is.

The first thing most people do about a cloud bill that has got out of hand is open a cost analysis blade and sort by spend. It is the obvious move and it is not wrong, but on its own it finds a fraction of what is there. A proper audit of the estate goes considerably further, and it is where most of the saving comes from. But there is a last tranche that no amount of auditing will surface on its own.

On one engagement with a global accountancy training organisation we removed £150,000 a year from an Azure bill. A meaningful part of that came from resources the tooling was perfectly happy with. They were running. They were healthy. They were the right size for what they were doing. The problem was that nobody needed them doing it.

What the tools can and cannot see

Azure Cost Management, Advisor and the various third-party FinOps products are good at a specific class of question. Is this virtual machine oversized for its observed load. Is this disk unattached. Is this reservation underused. Would a savings plan be cheaper than pay-as-you-go for this consumption pattern. Those are real savings and you should take all of them.

What no tool can answer is whether the thing should exist. A resource group spun up for a proof of concept that concluded two years ago looks exactly like a resource group doing valuable work. An integration runtime feeding a report nobody opens looks exactly like one feeding a report the board reads every Monday. The telemetry is identical. The difference is organisational, and it lives in people's heads.

So we ask

On that engagement the method was unglamorous. We interviewed the internal team and we interviewed the third parties who had built into the estate over the years, which on a long-lived environment is usually several firms who have each left something behind. The questions were simple and the same each time.

  • What did you build here, and is it still being used
  • Who asked for it, and are they still here
  • If this disappeared on Friday, who would ring you
  • What are you afraid to turn off, and why

That last one is the most productive question in cloud cost work. Fear of turning something off is almost never about the thing itself. It is about not knowing what depends on it. Once you can answer that, large amounts of spend become obviously removable, and the answer is usually a half-hour conversation rather than a month of dependency mapping.

Hundreds of orphaned resources came out of that estate. Not because a tool flagged them, but because somebody said "oh, that was for the migration, we finished that in 2023".

Do the tooling work too, in the right order

None of this is an argument against the technical work. On the same engagement we moved virtual machines onto reserved instances where the usage pattern justified it, and applied savings plans to app services. Those are mechanical, repeatable and worth real money.

But do them second. Commitment-based discounts on a resource you are about to delete are not a saving, they are a prepayment for waste with a one-year or three-year lock-in attached. The order matters: find out what should not exist, remove it, and only then commit to a discount on what is left.

The part that decides whether it lasts

A saving that nobody can maintain comes back. The team needs to be able to see the cost picture themselves, in a form they will actually open, or you have bought them twelve months and a repeat of the same engagement.

At a UK financial services firm we built more than fifteen Azure Workbooks for exactly this, alongside identifying around £250,000 a year of savings. The workbooks were not the deliverable anyone asked for and they are the reason the work held.

Questions this usually raises

How long does an interview-led review take?

Three days of read-only access is usually enough to get a ranked list with numbers against it. The interviews are a few hours in total; the time goes on verifying what people tell you against what the estate actually shows.

What if the people who built it have left?

That is common and it changes the method rather than defeating it. You move from interviews to artefacts: deployment pipelines, infrastructure as code if it exists, resource tags, activity logs and naming conventions. It is slower and it is still faster than guessing.

Reserved instances only pay off for workloads that are genuinely stable

James Dettmer, Principal 4 November 2026 2 min read

A one-year or three-year commitment on the wrong workload is not a discount. It is a prepayment for waste, with a lock-in attached.

Reserved instances and savings plans are the first thing people reach for when a cloud bill needs to come down, because the headline discount is large and the change looks free. You are not altering any architecture. You are not asking anyone to rewrite anything. You click a thing and the bill goes down.

That is true, and it is why the trap is so easy to walk into.

What you are actually buying

A reservation is a commitment to pay for a specific amount of a specific thing for one or three years whether or not you use it. The discount is the price of that certainty, from the provider's point of view. You are selling them predictability and they are paying you for it.

Which means the only question that matters is whether your usage is genuinely predictable, and over the whole term, not over the fortnight you happened to look at.

Three patterns that look stable and are not

The workload with a migration behind it. If anything in the estate is mid-move, from on-premise, between regions, or from virtual machines to containers, usage is stable right up until it is zero. Commit after the move, not during.

The environment that mirrors production. Development, UAT and pre-production environments often look like steady consumers because they are left running. They are also the first thing a team turns off when someone asks them to save money, which means your reservation and your cost-cutting initiative are now fighting each other.

The workload somebody is about to delete. This is the one that catches people who do the commitment work before the clear-out. We covered that order of operations separately, and it is worth repeating: find out what should not exist first.

What stable actually looks like

Domain controllers. Jump boxes. The database behind a line-of-business application that has been running for six years and will be running for six more. Integration runtimes that have a fixed job. The kind of thing where, if you ask the team whether it will still be there in three years, nobody hesitates.

On the engagement where we took £150,000 a year out of a global accountancy training organisation's bill, reservations were part of the answer, but only for the workloads that passed that test. The rest of the saving came from removing resources entirely and from savings plans on app services, which are more flexible and therefore safer where the pattern is less certain.

A practical rule

Before any commitment, ask who would be surprised if this workload disappeared in eighteen months. If the honest answer is nobody, or if it is a name followed by "but they are leaving", do not commit. Pay the on-demand rate for another quarter and find out.

The cost of being wrong is asymmetric. Paying on-demand for three extra months on something you later reserve costs you a little. Reserving three years of something you delete in six months costs you the whole commitment.

Questions this usually raises

Are savings plans safer than reserved instances?

Generally yes, because they commit you to an hourly spend rather than to a specific resource, so you keep the discount if the shape of the workload changes. They usually discount slightly less, which is the price of that flexibility.

Can you get out of a reservation?

Azure allows exchanges and, within limits, cancellations with a fee, and the rules have tightened over time. Treat it as a commitment you intend to keep rather than one you plan to unwind.
